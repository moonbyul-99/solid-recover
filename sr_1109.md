## Method 

### Motivation of adaptive-weighted clip loss 

The motivation of adaptive-weighted clip loss comes from a simple biological insights: Given a query cell, the differences between the query cell and different cell types should be different.

Suppose the query cell is $x = (x^{R},x^{A}) \in R^{r+a}$, and is belonged to cell type $C^{t}$. Then given cell $x_1$ also from $C^{t}$ and $x_{2}$ from different cell type $C^{q}$, then the relationship of differences between these two query pair should be 

$$ d(x,x_1) < d(x,x_2)$$

In original clip loss, we treated any embedding pairs from different cell pair as a negative pair, which ignores the internal biological structure. Given the query cell $x$ and its different modality embedding $(z^{R}, z^{A})$,  the clip loss maximize the difference between $x$ and any other cell $x_i$ while minimize the difference between its own two views $(z^A, z^R)$, i.e. 

$$
\begin{aligned}
& L^{a2r} = - \log \frac{sim(z^{A}, z^{R})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} sim(z^{A}, z_i^{R})} \\
& L^{r2a} = - \log \frac{sim(z^{R}, z^{A})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} sim(z^{R}, z_j^{A})} \\ 
& \min \mathcal{L} = \frac{\mathcal{L^{r2a}} + \mathcal{L^{a2r}}} {2}
\end{aligned}
$$ 

In order to take the biology structure into consideration, we proposed to distinguish the weights between different cell type pairs. Generally speaking, the differences should obey the following criteria:

$$
d(x,y) < d(x,z) \;\; \text{if} \;\;(x,y) \; \text{belongs to the same cell type} 
$$ 

Therefore, we fomulate the clip loss into adaptive-weighted clip loss 

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathcal{L^{a2r}} = - \log \frac{sim(z^{A}, z^{R})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_j*sim(z^{A}, z_i^{R})} 
\end{aligned}
$$

In practice, it is hard to estimate the cell-cell difference weights $w_{ij} \in \mathbf{R^{+}}$, we introduce a adaptive weight strategy. The details of the algorithms are listed as following:

1. compute the original similarity matrix $C = (C_{ij}) \in R^{n\times n}$, in which $C_{ij} = z^{A}_{i} \cdot z^{R}_j$
2. for each row, we split the column indices into 3 part. $\mathcal{N_l} \cup \mathcal{N_h}\cup \mathcal{N_m}$, which corresponds to low(l, the top-k weight), high(w, the last top-k weight), medium(m, other weight), confidence negative samples.
3. compute the row weighted clip loss 
   
   $$
     \mathcal{L^{a2r}} = - \log \frac{c_{ij}}{w_l*\sum_{i\in \mathcal{N_l}} c_{ij}+w_h*\sum_{i\in \mathcal{N_h}} sim(z^{A}, z_i^{R})+w_l*\sum_{i\in \mathcal{N_l}} sim(z^{A}, z_i^{R})} 
   $$
4. for each columns, perform the similar split and weighted sum.
5. average the row weighted clip loss and column weighted clip loss.

### Theoretical Analysis of the Adaptive-Weighted CLIP Loss

We argue that the adaptive-weighted CLIP loss not only incorporates biological structural priors but also enables more nuanced gradient control during training. 

To analyze its behavior, consider a single negative pair $(i, j)$; for brevity, we omit superscripts. The partial derivative of the standard CLIP loss $\mathcal{L}$ with respect to the similarity score $c_{ij}$ is:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial c_{ij}} &= \frac{\exp(c_{ij})}{T_{-j} + \exp(c_{ij})}, \\
\text{where} \quad T_{-j} &= \sum_{l \neq j} \exp(c_{il}).
\end{aligned}
$$

In the adaptive-weighted variant, denoted $\mathcal{L}^w$, suppose we adjust only the weight associated with pair $(i, j)$. The corresponding gradient becomes:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}^w}{\partial c_{ij}} = \frac{w_{ij} \exp(c_{ij})}{T_{-j} + w_{ij} \exp(c_{ij})}.
$$

It follows directly that:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{w_{ij} \exp(c_{ij})}{T_{-j} + w_{ij} \exp(c_{ij})} > \frac{\exp(c_{ij})}{T_{-j} + \exp(c_{ij})} \quad \text{if} \quad w_{ij} > 1, \\
&\frac{w_{ij} \exp(c_{ij})}{T_{-j} + w_{ij} \exp(c_{ij})} < \frac{\exp(c_{ij})}{T_{-j} + \exp(c_{ij})} \quad \text{if} \quad w_{ij} < 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently, the update rule for $c_{ij}$ under the weighted loss is:

$$
c_{ij}^{w\text{-new}} = c_{ij} - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}^w}{\partial c_{ij}}.
$$

- **For a negative pair $(i, j)$ that is actually similar** (i.e., a low-confidence negative sample), we set $w_{ij} < 1$. This yields a smaller gradient magnitude, leading to:
  
  $$
  c_{ij}^{w\text{-new}} > c_{ij}^{\text{new}},
  $$

  meaning the similarity score $c_{ij}$ decreases more slowly compared to the standard CLIP update.

- **For a truly dissimilar pair $(i, j)$** (i.e., a high-confidence negative sample), we set $w_{ij} > 1$. This results in a larger gradient magnitude, so:

  $$
  c_{ij}^{w\text{-new}} < c_{ij}^{\text{new}},
  $$

  causing the similarity $c_{ij}$ to decrease more rapidly than in the original CLIP formulation.

This adaptive weighting mechanism thus allows the model to modulate learning dynamics based on the reliability of negative samples, promoting more robust representation learning.
